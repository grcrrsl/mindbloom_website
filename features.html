Self-care toolkit

Coping strategies that actually work for students

These strategies are backed by research. None replace professional help — but they can make a meaningful difference, even during the most overwhelming stretch of your semester.

Mindfulness

Paying attention to the present moment without judgment. Even 5 minutes between lectures — no app required — can help reduce stress and exam anxiety.

Physical movement

Movement releases mood-boosting chemicals and helps lower stress hormones. You don't need a gym membership — even a 20-minute walk around campus can make a noticeable difference.

Journaling

Writing down your thoughts — even messy, unfinished thoughts — can help process emotions and reduce the mental overload that comes with juggling responsibilities.

Sleep hygiene

Poor sleep can worsen anxiety, concentration, and memory. Consistent sleep and a realistic wind-down routine can meaningfully improve mood, focus, and learning.

Social connection

Even brief, genuine connection — a real conversation with a classmate, lunch with a friend, checking in with someone you trust — can help buffer stress, loneliness, and overwhelm.

Breathing exercises

Slow breathing activates your body's calming system. Try the 4-7-8 method before exams, presentations, or difficult conversations: inhale 4 seconds, hold 7, exhale 8.

Creative expression

Drawing, music, writing, cooking, or making something new can give your brain a break from constant problem-solving and help process emotions in a different way.

Nature exposure

Even short breaks outdoors — under trees, near water, or anywhere green — can lower stress levels and restore attention. Campus parks count.

Problem-solving

When a semester feels crushing, shrink the problem. One subject, one task, one hour at a time. Regaining a sense of control can itself be therapeutic.

Professional support

Counselors, therapists, psychiatrists, and campus wellness services exist for a reason. Reaching out isn't giving up — it's using available support when coping alone isn't enough.

Medication

When appropriately prescribed and monitored, psychiatric medication can be an effective part of treatment. It's not a last resort for everyone — and for some people, it makes other coping strategies more effective.

Social support

Your barkada, blockmates, online communities, mentors, or support groups can matter more than you think. Being understood by people who “get it” can make difficult periods feel less isolating.

Self-Check

Check in with yourself

These short check-ins aren't diagnostic tools — but they can help you notice patterns, reflect on how you're doing, and decide whether additional support might help. For a proper assessment, consult a qualified professional. Building the habit of checking in with yourself matters.

Daily Mood Check-In

A quick 5-question pulse check on how you're doing today.

Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you felt little interest or pleasure in doing things?

How often have you felt down, depressed, or hopeless?

How often have you had trouble falling or staying asleep?

How often have you felt tired or had little energy?

How often have you felt nervous, anxious, or on edge?

This is a self-check only — not a clinical diagnosis. If you're concerned, please speak with a mental health professional.

Honest answers to the questions students are often too afraid to ask — about therapy, medication, and knowing when to reach out.

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who can diagnose mental health conditions and prescribe medication. A psychologist holds a doctoral degree in psychology and provides assessment and therapy but typically cannot prescribe medication (with some exceptions). A therapist or counsellor is a broader term covering licensed professionals (LCSWs, LMFTs, LPCs) who provide talk therapy. Many people work with both a psychiatrist for medication management and a therapist for ongoing support.

Friends are invaluable for support, but therapy offers something different: a trained professional who provides a confidential, non-judgmental space and evidence-based techniques. Consider therapy if your distress is persistent (lasting weeks), interfering with daily life, work, or relationships, or if you feel your friends are burdened. You don't need to be in crisis to benefit from therapy — many people use it for personal growth and self-understanding.

When properly prescribed and monitored, psychiatric medication is safe and effective. The goal of medication is not to change who you are — it's to reduce symptoms so you can function and feel more like yourself. All medications have potential side effects, which is why regular check-ins with your prescribing doctor are essential. Many people find that medication, combined with therapy, gives the best outcomes.

Find Support

Who Can Help You — and How

Knowing who does what makes it easier to find the right support — whether you're dealing with burnout, anxiety, academic pressure, or something deeper. The right help isn't one-size-fits-all, and you deserve care that matches what you're experiencing.

Psychiatrist

MD / DO / Mental Health Physician

A medical doctor specializing in mental health. Psychiatrists can diagnose mental health conditions, prescribe medication, and provide treatment. In the Philippines, you may find psychiatrists in hospitals, private clinics, and institutions like the National Center for Mental Health (NCMH).

Good for: Medication management, complex diagnoses, severe conditions

Psychologist

RPsy / MA / PhD / PsyD

A licensed mental health professional trained in psychological assessment, diagnosis, and therapy. Psychologists can help with anxiety, depression, ADHD, trauma, learning concerns, and emotional difficulties.

Good for: Assessment, CBT, trauma therapy, diagnostic clarity

Licensed Therapist

RPm / Counseling Professional / Psychotherapist

Provides talk therapy and emotional support for concerns like anxiety, burnout, academic pressure, relationship stress, and life transitions. Often a good starting point for students seeking mental health support.

Good for: Talk therapy, stress management, emotional support, student concerns

Guidance Counsellor

School / University Counseling Services

Provides support for academic stress, adjustment challenges, career concerns, family issues, and student well-being. Many colleges and universities in the Philippines offer free or low-cost guidance and counseling services.

Good for: Academic pressure, career concerns, adjustment difficulties, campus support

Social Worker

RSW (Registered Social Worker)

Helps individuals and families connect with community services, crisis support, financial assistance programs, and mental health resources. They may also provide counseling depending on their role and training.

Good for: Community resources, crisis intervention, family support, care navigation

Peer Support / Support Groups

Community-Based & Student Support

Peer support comes from people with lived experience who provide understanding, encouragement, and connection. This can include student organizations, campus support groups, online communities, and recovery groups.

Good for: Shared experiences, reducing isolation, community connection, recovery support

Common Therapy Approaches Explained

CBT
Cognitive Behavioural Therapy

Identifies and reshapes thought patterns that contribute to anxiety, depression, procrastination, and avoidance. Structured, practical, and often short-term — making it popular among students looking for actionable tools.

EMDR
Eye Movement Desensitisation & Reprocessing

Uses guided bilateral stimulation (such as eye movements or tapping) to help process traumatic or distressing experiences. Commonly used for trauma, PTSD, and overwhelming life events.

DBT
Dialectical Behaviour Therapy

Teaches emotional regulation, distress tolerance, mindfulness, and healthier relationship skills. Helpful for people dealing with intense emotions, impulsivity, or chronic emotional overwhelm.

ACT
Acceptance & Commitment Therapy

Encourages accepting difficult thoughts and feelings rather than constantly fighting them — while taking actions aligned with your values. Especially helpful for anxiety, perfectionism, and avoidance cycles common in student life.

IFS
Internal Family Systems

Explores the different “parts” of yourself — such as your inner critic, perfectionist, anxious self, or withdrawn self — to build self-understanding, healing, and self-compassion.

MBCT
Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy

Combines mindfulness practices with cognitive therapy techniques to reduce rumination, stress, and relapse into depression. Helpful for students who struggle with overthinking or persistent worry.
